# Research Memo

## Title
Commercial Real Estate Office Cycles and Distributional Effects

## Public site research question
**Who wins and loses when office values crash?**

When office values reprice after a demand shock, who wins and who loses — across cities,
asset classes, and investor types — and what does the San Francisco / Manhattan /
Washington, D.C. gap reveal about how distributed the pain is?

*(This is the question on the capstone website at econ30-lab.vercel.app. The memo below
uses a closely related framing for the full research pipeline.)*

## Extended research question (memo / pipeline)
How do boom-and-bust cycles in office markets redistribute risk, wealth, and
opportunity across institutional investors, small business tenants, and urban
workers?

## Motivation
Commercial real estate is one of the largest asset classes in the economy.
Large swings in occupancy, rents, values, and financing conditions can shift
economic burdens unevenly across stakeholder groups and may amplify inequality.
National "office crisis" headlines hide a San Francisco crisis — and a Manhattan split.

## Scope
- Geographic focus: San Francisco, Manhattan, and Washington, D.C.
- Asset focus: office segment
- Channels:
  - Asset valuations and REIT exposure (institutional)
  - Rent and demand pressure (small business tenants)
  - Employment sensitivity to office-market stress (urban workers)

## Data Inputs
- Hand-curated city panel: vacancy, Class A asking rent, sublease (2015–2024 Q4)
- FRED national series: CRE loans and delinquency rates
- Primary PDFs: Colliers (SF), Cushman / NYC Comptroller (Manhattan), D.C. ORA / JLL

## Empirical Strategy
1. Build a harmonized three-city panel from primary brokerage and government reports.
2. Compare adjustment paths: demand collapse (SF), K-shaped flight-to-quality (Manhattan), fiscal drag (D.C.).
3. Pair city distress with FRED bank-exposure series.
4. Translate patterns into explicit winners/losers and progress-studies framing.

## Preliminary Interpretation Framework
- Falling office values can reduce investor wealth while also easing rent pressure
  for some tenants — but workers and municipal budgets often feel the shock first.
- Elevated vacancy coincides with weaker local labor demand for office-adjacent services.
- Net distributional effects depend on balance-sheet exposure, bargaining power,
  and labor-market flexibility.
- Progress lens: repricing is an engine for adaptive reuse; bottlenecks are zoning,
  conversion economics, and tax-base dependence on office assessments.

## Next Steps
- Expand robustness checks for alternative cycle definitions.
- Add net effective rent where available (concessions are hidden in asking rent).
- Track conversion pipeline outcomes (potential vs. realized wins).
