## Beat 1 — Motivation (~90 sec)

### Slide 1 (20 sec) — open in `/presentation`

> “After COVID, remote work pulled demand out of offices. Vacancy roughly tripled. Hundreds of billions in value were repriced. But when people say ‘office crisis,’ they’re often talking about one national headline — and my project shows that headline hides very different local stories.”

### Slide 2 (20 sec)

> “San Francisco vacancy hit about **37%**. Manhattan trophy rents actually **rose** while vacancy stayed elevated. Washington, D.C. faces a slower burn but real fiscal risk. Same national shock — three adjustment paths.”

**Read thesis line:**

> “National ‘office crisis’ headlines hide a San Francisco crisis — and a Manhattan split.”

### Switch to live site — Page 1 (50 sec)

Scroll to hero + stat strip. Point at:

- **19.6%** U.S. office vacancy (Q1 2025)  
- **24%** Moody’s peak scenario  
- **$556.8B** illustrative nationwide value destruction  

> “These numbers tell us the shock is real. But they don’t tell us *who* bears it. Downtown workers in food, retail, and cleaning. Small businesses tied to office foot traffic. City budgets that depend on property taxes. And on the other side — institutional buyers who can purchase distressed towers at a discount.”

**Puzzle (tension):**

> “The puzzle is distribution. Manhattan Class A rents went up. San Francisco vacancy went up seven times more than D.C. If you only read national news, you miss that completely.”

## Beat 2 — Your question (~30 sec)

**Stop at H1 on hero. Say slowly:**

> “**Who wins and loses when office values crash?**”

Pause.

> “That’s the one question this site answers. Not a literature review — when office values reprice after a demand shock, who gains option value, and who bears the fiscal and employment cost? The SF / NYC / D.C. gap is how I show the pain isn’t evenly distributed.”

## Beat 3 — Data and sources (~60 sec)

**Scroll to “What this project adds” (Page 1) or use Slide 4.**

> “Three data layers, plain language:”

1. **City panel** — “I hand-curated annual Q4 numbers from 2015 to 2024 for San Francisco, Manhattan, and D.C. — vacancy, Class A asking rent, and sublease space. Sources: Colliers, NYC Comptroller / Cushman, D.C. Office of Revenue Analysis.”

2. **National banking** — “Two live FRED series: commercial real estate loans on bank balance sheets, and CRE delinquency rates. City pain in the charts; national credit exposure down here.”

3. **Literature anchor** — “Gupta and co-authors estimate a long-run **46%** decline in NYC office values. SPUR and the NYC Comptroller frame policy choices — doom loop vs. boom loop.”

> “How I used it: I harmonized three cities on the same metrics so we compare adjustment *paths*, not just read one national headline. Brokerage figures vary by a few points across vendors — these comparisons are directional, not precise.”

## Beat 4 — Interactive (~2 min 30 sec) — **show, don’t tell**

**Navigate to Page 2 dashboard.** Compare all should load by default.

### Step 1 — Vacancy, Compare all (45 sec)

> “Start with Compare all on vacancy. Coral is San Francisco, gold is Manhattan, teal is D.C. Watch SF diverge — from about **5%** to **37%** by 2024. Manhattan and D.C. rise, but modestly. SF gained **31 percentage points**; D.C. gained about **4**. National crisis, local catastrophe.”

Point at annotation if visible: “SF +31 pp vs DC +4 pp.”

### Step 2 — Rent, Compare all (45 sec)

> “Same view, Class A asking rent. Manhattan’s gold line **rises** through the shock. San Francisco **falls**. That’s flight-to-quality — tenants consolidating into trophy buildings while older stock absorbs vacancy. A K-shaped recovery inside one market.”

### Step 3 — San Francisco single-city, sublease (30 sec)

Click **San Francisco**. Scroll to sublease panel.

> “Sublease spiked before direct vacancy in all three cities — tenants putting space back on the market before landlords feel it in headline vacancy. An early distress signal.”

### Step 4 — Finding #01 (optional, 15 sec)

Scroll to findings grid.

> “Finding one states it directly: the ‘office shock’ is really a San Francisco shock. The interactive isn’t decoration — it’s how I prove the headline is wrong.”

## Beat 5 — Takeaways + next steps (~90 sec)

**Scroll to Page 3 (`#page-3`).**

### Winners / Losers (45 sec)

> “Winners: institutional investors and well-capitalized buyers at a distressed basis; trophy landlords in Manhattan who held headline rents; asset managers with patience to wait out rollover.”

> “Losers: urban workers in office-adjacent services — food, retail, cleaning, security; small businesses on downtown corridors; municipal budgets when assessed values and transaction volume weaken.”

### Mini case study (15 sec)

> “Concrete example: SF office-to-residential conversion. A distressed seller exits; a conversion buyer captures rezoning upside; the city gets housing and stabilizes tax base. Progress for some — pain for the seller forced to exit.”

### One limit (15 sec)

> “One limit: Class A *asking* rent isn’t net effective rent — concessions are often hidden. And conversion wins are potential, not yet at scale.”

### Close (15 sec)

**Thesis line again:**

> “National ‘office crisis’ headlines hide a San Francisco crisis — and a Manhattan split. The winners have capital and patience; the losers are workers, small businesses, and cities tied to the old office equilibrium.”

### Next 10 days (15 sec)

> “If I had ten more days: add net effective rent where available, deepen the D.C. fiscal case study, and keep rehearsing this demo until it’s under eight minutes.”
