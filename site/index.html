01 — The numbers

Original research — progress in real estate

Who wins and loses when office values crash?

By Reade Decker · Original research project

Vacancy roughly tripled in five years and hundreds of billions in office value were repriced — but the shock looks very different in San Francisco, New York, and Washington, D.C. This project asks what kind of progress hides inside that repricing: who gains option value when offices are forced to evolve.

About this project

Context

U.S. office vacancy is at multi-decade highs; Moody's projects a peak around 24% in 2026. Tech-heavy downtowns and large monocentric markets absorb very different hits — with knock-on effects on tax bases, foot traffic, and lender balance sheets.

Literature

Lease-level work by Gupta, Mittal, and Van Nieuwerburgh (NYC ~46% long-run decline), Moody's vacancy forecasts, Cushman & Wakefield and Colliers market reports, the NYC Comptroller's doom-loop framing, and J.P. Morgan on bank CRE exposure.

Research question

When office values reprice after a demand shock, who wins and who loses — across cities, asset classes, and investor types — and what does the SF / NYC / D.C. gap reveal about how distributed the pain is?

Why this is progress

Forced repricing reallocates capital, space, and people from saturated office use to higher-value ones — conversions, lab buildouts, mixed-use downtowns, and a cheaper basis for adaptive reuse.

Why these three cities?

Same national shock, three adjustment paths — tech demand collapse, scale + flight-to-quality, and government-adjacent fiscal exposure.

San Francisco

Tech/WFH shock and extreme vacancy — a demand-collapse story where conversion and repricing dominate.

Manhattan

Scale plus flight-to-quality — a K-shaped recovery where trophy Class A holds while older stock absorbs vacancy.

Washington, D.C.

Government-adjacent demand with rising vacant stock — fiscal and property-tax exposure as assessed values weaken.

Newest, highest-quality buildings; trophy = top-tier Class A in prime locations.
Asking rent
Listed rent before concessions — not net effective rent.
Sublease
Tenant re-letting space they no longer need — an early distress signal.
Flight to quality
Tenants downsizing into better buildings, leaving older stock empty.
K-shaped
Top-tier assets recover while lower-tier assets stay distressed.

Case study: San Francisco

Tech-heavy remote adoption hit San Francisco early. By Q4 2024, Colliers reports vacancy at 36.6% — up from 5.2% at end-2019. Sublease availability peaked near 9 M sq ft.

Class A asking rents softened from ~$80/sf to ~$70/sf — landlords prefer concessions over headline cuts. SPUR argues recovery requires conversions and downtown activation, not just market time.

Losers here

  • Downtown retailers and service businesses tied to office foot traffic
  • Municipal tax base as assessed values and transaction volume weaken
  • Class B/C landlords forced to cut rents and offer heavy concessions

Winners (potential)

  • Conversion developers buying distressed office at a discount
  • Value buyers and distressed-asset funds at repriced basis

Case study: New York City

Manhattan splits in two. Overall vacancy peaked ~18–19% in 2022, then drifted toward 17–18% in 2024 as trophy Class A leasing recovered — the NYC Comptroller's "doom loop vs. boom loop" framing.

Class A asking rents rose ($83/sf → $86/sf) even as vacancy stayed elevated. Tenants consolidated into fewer premium floors; landlords held headline rents while hiding concessions off-lease — a classic K-shaped pattern the vacancy aggregate misses.

Losers here

  • Class B/C owners and older fringe stock carrying vacancy
  • Transit-adjacent small businesses when weekday foot traffic thins

Winners here

  • Trophy landlords who raised headline rents through the shock
  • Institutional buyers of discounted fringe assets with conversion optionality

Findings from the real city panel

Four patterns from the city dashboard and FRED series above. City deltas compare Q4 2019 to Q4 2024.

  1. 01 · real data

    The "office shock" is really a San Francisco shock.

    Vacancy jumps: SF +31.4 pp, Manhattan +6.5 pp, DC +4.2 pp. SF's shock is roughly seven times DC's — national headlines hide a lopsided geography.

  2. 02 · real data

    Trophy-class rents diverge from vacancy.

    Class A asking rents: SF $80 → $70 (−12%), Manhattan $83 → $86 (+4%), DC flat. Manhattan raised trophy rents through the shock as flight-to-quality concentrated demand at the top — a K-shaped pattern vacancy alone misses.

  3. 03 · real data

    Sublease is the leading indicator.

    Sublease peaked SF +5×, Manhattan ~2×, DC ~2×. It ran up before direct vacancy and is now slowly bleeding back — an early sign the worst may be behind us.

  4. 04 · real data

    Banks absorb the shock — so far.

    CRE loans grew $2.32T → $3.01T (Dec 2019–2024, +29.5%). Delinquencies rose 0.67% → 1.56% — more than double, but still a small share of book. Repricing is real; the official credit event is, so far, contained.

03 — Takeaways

Mini case study: SF office-to-residential conversion

When a tech tenant vacates a 1960s tower at a distressed basis, the owner faces a choice: hold for years of negative cash flow or sell to a conversion specialist. Public reporting on projects like 550 California Street shows how a discounted office sale can become housing supply — the seller exits a losing asset, the buyer captures rezoning upside, and the city stabilizes a shrinking tax base.

That is the "who wins" story hiding inside SF's vacancy spike: not abstract capital, but a concrete transfer from distressed sellers to patient buyers with a different use case.

Who wins and who loses

Winners

  • Institutional investors and well-capitalized buyers acquiring distressed or discounted assets
  • Owners of Class A / trophy buildings in prime locations (relative resilience)
  • Asset managers with long horizons who can wait out lease rollover and repricing

Losers

  • Small businesses in downtown corridors tied to office foot traffic
  • Urban workers in office-adjacent services when attendance and hiring slow
  • Municipal budgets when assessed values and transaction volume weaken

Capital that can buy at a discount and hold — or restructure — captures option value when others are forced to sell or refinance.

What comes next

  • Office-to-residential conversion (where economically and legally feasible)
  • A wall of CRE debt maturities and refinancing under higher rates
  • Fiscal feedback loops: weaker downtowns → fewer services → harder recovery

The repricing is uneven by design: SF absorbed the demand shock, Manhattan split by asset class, and D.C. faces fiscal drag. The winners are those with capital and patience; the losers are workers, small businesses, and cities tied to the old office equilibrium.
