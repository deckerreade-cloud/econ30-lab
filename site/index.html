01 — The numbers

Original research — progress in real estate

Who wins and loses when office values crash?

By Reade Decker · Original research project

The post-pandemic office shock is one of the largest real-economy reallocations of the 2020s. Vacancy roughly tripled in five years and hundreds of billions in office value were repriced — but the same shock looks very different in San Francisco, New York, and Washington, D.C. This project combines a quantitative panel, interactive visualization, and narrative exposition to ask what kind of progress hides inside that repricing: which cities, capital pools, and uses gain when offices are forced to evolve.

About this project

Context

U.S. office vacancy is at multi-decade highs and Moody's projects a peak around 24% in 2026. The shock is uneven: tech-heavy downtowns (San Francisco) and large monocentric office markets (Manhattan, Washington D.C.) absorb very different hits, with knock-on effects on city tax bases, small business foot traffic, and lender balance sheets.

Literature

The project draws on lease-level work by Gupta, Mittal, and Van Nieuwerburgh on long-run office value declines (a leading estimate puts NYC at ~46%), Moody's Analytics vacancy forecasts, Cushman & Wakefield and Colliers market reports, the NYC Comptroller's doom-loop / boom-loop framing, and J.P. Morgan research on bank exposure to commercial real estate.

Research question

When office values reprice after a demand shock, who actually wins and who loses — across cities, asset classes, and investor types — and what does the gap between San Francisco, New York, and Washington, D.C. reveal about how distributed the pain really is?

Why this is progress

Forced repricing is uncomfortable, but it's also how capital, space, and people get reallocated from a saturated use to higher-value ones — office-to-residential conversions, life-sciences and lab buildouts, denser mixed-use downtowns, and a cheaper cost basis for adaptive reuse. I'm excited about this because it's a generational chance to rebuild downtowns instead of merely defending them.

Case study: San Francisco

Tech-heavy remote adoption hit San Francisco early and hard. By Q4 2024, Colliers reports overall office vacancy at 36.6% — up from 5.2% at the end of 2019, a roughly seven-fold increase. Sublease availability ballooned from under 2 M sq ft to nearly 9 M sq ft at the post-pandemic peak.

Class A asking rents have softened from a 2019 peak around $80/sf to about $70/sf — a real but contained drop, because landlords prefer concessions over headline rent cuts. Policy voices (SPUR, the Mayor's office) argue recovery requires conversions and downtown activation, not just market time.

Case study: New York City

Manhattan's market splits in two. Overall vacancy peaked around 18-19% in 2022, then drifted back toward 17-18% in 2024 as trophy Class A leasing recovered. The NYC Comptroller frames this as "doom loop vs. boom loop" — both can coexist inside one market.

Importantly, Manhattan Class A asking rents kept rising ($83/sf in 2019 → $86/sf in 2024) even as vacancy stayed elevated. Trophy buildings absorbed the flight-to-quality bid while Class B/C and older fringe stock did the heavy lifting on vacancy and sublease.

Findings from the real city panel

All four findings use the real data shown above — site/data/city_panel.json for the cities and site/data/fred_real.json for the national series. City deltas compare Q4 2019 to Q4 2024.

  1. 01 · real data

    The "office shock" is really a San Francisco shock.

    Q4 2019 → Q4 2024 vacancy jumps (Colliers / Cushman / DC ORA): SF +31.4 pp (5.2% → 36.6%), Manhattan +6.5 pp (11.0% → 17.5%), DC +4.2 pp (17.4% → 21.6%). SF's shock is roughly seven times DC's. National "office in distress" headlines hide a very lopsided geography.

  2. 02 · real data

    Trophy-class rents diverge from vacancy.

    Class A asking rents moved SF $80 → $70 (-12%), Manhattan $83 → $86 (+4%), DC $58 → $58 (flat). Manhattan landlords actually raised trophy rents through the shock as flight-to-quality concentrated demand at the top of the stack — a classic K-shaped pattern that the vacancy aggregate misses.

  3. 03 · real data

    Sublease, not direct vacancy, is the leading indicator.

    Sublease space peaked SF +5x (1.8 → 9.4 M sq ft), Manhattan ~2x (11.3 → 21.5 M sq ft), DC ~2x (2.6 → 4.6 M sq ft). Sublease ran up well before direct vacancy and is now slowly bleeding back down in all three cities — an early-but-real sign the worst is behind us.

  4. 04 · real data

    Banks are absorbing the shock, not unwinding from it.

    From FRED CREACBM027NBOG: CRE loans on US commercial-bank balance sheets grew from $2.32 trillion (Dec 2019) to $3.01 trillion (Dec 2024), a +29.5% expansion. Delinquencies (DRCRELEXFACBS) rose from 0.67% to 1.56% — more than double, but still a small share of book. The repricing is real; the official credit event is, so far, contained.
