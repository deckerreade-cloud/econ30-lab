Illustrative value destruction

After COVID, remote work pulled demand out of offices. Vacancy roughly tripled. But “the office crisis” is not one story — it is a distribution story.

The puzzle

Same shock. Three cities. Three paths.

  • San Francisco — vacancy ~5% → ~37% (demand collapse)
  • Manhattan — trophy rents rose while vacancy stayed high (K-shaped split)
  • Washington, D.C. — slower burn, fiscal / tax-base exposure

National “office crisis” headlines hide a San Francisco crisis — and a Manhattan split.

Research question

Who wins and loses when office values crash?

When office values reprice after a demand shock, who gains option value — and who bears the fiscal and employment cost?

Data & sources

What I built on

  • City panel — hand-curated Q4 data 2015–2024 (SF, Manhattan, D.C.): vacancy, Class A rent, sublease
  • FRED — CRE loans + delinquency on U.S. bank balance sheets
  • Literature — Gupta et al. (~46% NYC long-run decline); NYC Comptroller; SPUR; D.C. ORA

Harmonized three cities on the same metrics — compare paths, not just headlines.

Takeaways

Winners vs. losers

  • Winners — patient capital at distressed basis; trophy landlords (Manhattan)
  • Losers — downtown workers in office-adjacent services; city tax bases
